# Ancient Rome 3: Hannibal over the Alps: fact check

| Claim | Fact | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Around three dozen war elephants | 37 (Appian) / 38 at the Rhône (Polybius) ✓ | Wikipedia "Hannibal"; Polybius III.42–46; romanempiretimes.com |
| Exact pass unknown | ✓ (Traversette, Clapier, Little St Bernard, Montgenèvre all argued) | Wikipedia "Hannibal's crossing of the Alps"; Mahaney et al. 2016 |
| Started from Cartagena (New Carthage), Carthaginian base in Spain; Carthage near today's Tunis | ✓ | Wikipedia "Hannibal"; Wikipedia "Cartagena" |
| Rome had won the First Punic War at sea (Aegates 241 BC), so land route in 218 BC | ✓ | Wikipedia "First Punic War"; Wikipedia "Hannibal" |
| Route: Pyrenees, southern Gaul, Rhône, Alps; over 1,500 km | Polybius gives ~9,000 stades (~1,600 km) New Carthage → Italy ✓ | Polybius III.39; Wikipedia "Hannibal's crossing of the Alps" |
| Rhône: rafts covered with soil so elephants thought they were on land | ✓ | Polybius III.46; Wikipedia "Hannibal's crossing of the Alps" |
| Late autumn, snow, landslides, attacks by mountain tribes; ~15 days in the Alps | ✓ | Polybius III.56; Livy XXI.38; Wikipedia "Hannibal's crossing of the Alps" |
| ~59,000 after the Pyrenees (50,000 foot + 9,000 horse) → 26,000 in Italy (20,000 + 6,000) | Polybius figures ✓ | Polybius III.35, III.56; Wikipedia "Hannibal" |
| Most elephants died in the first winter; Hannibal rode the last one through the flooded (Arno) marshes; lost an eye to infection | ✓ | Polybius III.79; Livy XXII.2; Wikipedia "Hannibal" |
| Beat three Roman armies in three years: Trebia 218, Trasimene 217, Cannae 216 BC | ✓ | Wikipedia "Second Punic War"; Wikipedia "Battle of Cannae" |
| Cannae (2 August 216 BC): encircled a bigger army; ~50,000 Romans killed in one day | Livy 48,200 killed; Polybius 70,000 ✓ | Wikipedia "Battle of Cannae"; Livy XXII.49 |
| Hannibal never besieged Rome; Rome invaded Africa; Scipio won at Zama, 202 BC | ✓ (only a brief march on Rome in 211 BC) | Wikipedia "Battle of Zama"; Wikipedia "Hannibal" |
| Horse-dung layer dated to c. 200 BC below the Col de la Traversette | ✓ (debated) | Mahaney et al., *Archaeometry* 59 (2017); The Conversation (2016); Live Science (2016) |
