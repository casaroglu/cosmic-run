# Ancient Rome 8: Roman concrete, roads and aqueducts: fact check

| Claim | Fact | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Roman marine concrete has survived ~2,000 years in seawater; modern concrete in seawater deteriorates within decades | ✓ | Wikipedia "Roman concrete"; Jackson et al., *American Mineralogist* (2017) |
| Pantheon dome, dedicated c. AD 126 under Hadrian; world's largest unreinforced concrete dome | ✓ | Wikipedia "Pantheon, Rome"; Wikipedia "Roman concrete" |
| Dome ~43.3 m across, height to oculus ~43.3 m; a sphere fits inside; no steel | ✓ | Wikipedia "Pantheon, Rome"; Britannica "Pantheon" |
| Volcanic ash (pozzolana, Pozzuoli near Naples) + lime | ✓ | Wikipedia "Roman concrete"; Vitruvius, *De architectura* II.6 |
| 2023 MIT-led study: hot mixing with quicklime → lime clasts that reseal cracks; lab samples healed within two weeks | ✓ | Seymour, Masic et al., *Science Advances* 9 (Jan 2023); MIT News "Riddle solved" (2023); Dezeen |
| Modern reinforced concrete cracks when embedded steel corrodes and expands | ✓ | Wikipedia "Reinforced concrete" (corrosion); Wikipedia "Concrete degradation" |
| 400,000+ km of roads, 80,500+ km stone-paved | ✓ | Wikipedia "Roman roads"; Britannica "Roman road system" |
| Via Appia begun 312 BC (Appius Claudius Caecus), Rome to Brundisium; still walkable in parts | ✓ | Wikipedia "Appian Way"; Livy IX.29 |
| Road layers: trench, rubble, gravel, concrete, paving stones | ✓ | Wikipedia "Roman roads"; Vitruvius VII.1 |
| Aqueducts ran on gravity; Pont du Gard (1st c. AD), southern France | ✓ | Wikipedia "Roman aqueduct"; Wikipedia "Pont du Gard" |
| Aqua Virgo, 19 BC by Agrippa; restored as Acqua Vergine, feeds the Trevi Fountain | ✓ | Wikipedia "Aqua Virgo"; Wikipedia "Trevi Fountain" |
