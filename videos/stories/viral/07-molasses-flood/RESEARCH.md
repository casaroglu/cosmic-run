# 07 — The Great Molasses Flood, Boston (1919): fact check

| Claim | Fact | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 1919, wave of molasses killed 21 in Boston | 21 killed ✓ | Wikipedia "Great Molasses Flood" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boston_molasses_disaster); History.com "Why the Great Molasses Flood Was So Deadly" (https://www.history.com/articles/great-molasses-flood-science); NBC News (https://www.nbcnews.com/news/us-news/great-boston-molasses-flood-1919-killed-21-after-2-million-n958326) |
| 15 January 1919, Boston's North End | ~12:30 p.m., 529 Commercial Street ✓ | Wikipedia; NBC News; History.com |
| A 50-foot tank | 50 ft tall, 90 ft diameter ✓ (a History.com caption says 58 ft — not used) | Wikipedia; History.com; NBC News |
| Over two million gallons | 2.3 million gallons (Wikipedia, NBC); capacity 2.5 million (History.com) → "over two million" ✓ | Wikipedia; NBC News; History.com |
| It leaked, and they painted it brown | Painted brown to hide leaks ✓ | Wikipedia; NBC News |
| It burst | Tank ruptured ✓ | Wikipedia; NBC News |
| Wave reportedly 25 feet high | 25 ft (Wikipedia, NBC); memorial plaque says 40 ft; History.com "up to 15 feet deep" → "reportedly" | Wikipedia; NBC News |
| About 35 miles an hour | Estimated 35 mph; Harvard (Nicole Sharp) modelling supports it ✓ | Wikipedia; History.com; NBC News |
| Cooling molasses thickened, trapping people | Became more viscous as it cooled; victims stuck ✓ | Wikipedia; History.com; NBC News |
| About 150 hurt | ~150 injured ✓ | Wikipedia; History.com; NBC News |
| Boston then required engineers to sign off on plans | Engineers'/architects' calculations filed and stamped drawings signed; became standard practice (NBC); licensed architect + engineer oversight (Wikipedia) ✓ | Wikipedia; NBC News |
| Smell reportedly lingered for decades | ✓ ("said to linger" / "lingered for decades") | Wikipedia; History.com |

**Softened:** wave height ("reportedly 25 feet"; sources range 15–40 ft); tank volume as "over two million gallons". **Dropped:** the $628,000 settlement (only Wikipedia), Prohibition-rush theory (Wikipedia calls it an assertion), horses killed (Wikipedia only), elevated-railway damage kept only as a visual (Wikipedia + City of Boston Archives photo).
